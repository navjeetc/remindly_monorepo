## What they do well, and where they stop

Once you have decided your parent needs reminding about their tablets, the next
question is what does the reminding. There is a whole category of device for
this — talking reminder clocks — and they are genuinely good. Before I describe
what Remindly does differently, it is worth being straight about what they do
better.

## What a talking reminder clock is

It looks like a chunky bedside clock with a large display. You record a message
in your own voice, set a time, and at that time it speaks the message out loud.

The best known is **Reminder Rosie**, which at the time of writing costs about
$150. It holds up to 25 recorded reminders, can be operated almost entirely by
voice, runs on mains power with battery backup, and — the part that matters —
needs **no wifi and no subscription**. Less expensive talking clocks exist from
other makers if 25 reminders and voice control are more than you need.

## What they are genuinely good at

**Your voice, not a robot's.** You record the message yourself. For a parent
with memory problems, hearing their daughter's actual voice say "Mum, it's time
for your tablets" lands differently from a synthesised one. This is not a small
thing and no app I know of does it better.

**Nothing to keep running.** No tab to leave open, no browser, no screen to
sleep. It is an object on a shelf. It works in a power cut, it works when the
broadband is down, and it will still be working in five years.

**No account, no password, no internet.** For a family that finds technology
stressful — or a parent who would be unsettled by a screen — this is the whole
argument. One purchase and it is done.

**It can be operated by voice.** For someone with poor eyesight, that matters
more than any feature on this page.

If your parent has dementia, if the house has unreliable internet, or if screens
confuse or upset them, **a talking clock is very likely the right answer** and
you should stop reading here and go and buy one.

## Where they stop

There is one thing a talking clock cannot do, and it is the thing that sends
most people looking for something else.

**It tells nobody.** It speaks into the room. Whether your mother heard it,
whether she was in the kitchen at the time, whether she took the tablets or
walked past — the clock does not know and cannot tell you. From your end,
nothing has changed. You are still ringing at nine to ask, and she is still
tired of being asked.

**You have to be in the room to change anything.** New prescription, changed
dose, appointment moved? You record the new message at the device. If you live
two hours away, a change to the routine means a visit or a phone call talking
someone through it.

**Twenty-five reminders is a ceiling.** Generous for most people, and worth
knowing if the routine is complicated.

## The other kind

The alternative is a reminder that speaks at their end **and reports back to
yours**. You set the times from your own phone, wherever you are. It speaks
aloud at the time. Your parent presses **Done** — one large button — or they do
not, and either way you find out without asking.

That last part is the whole difference. The question "did you take your
tablets?" stops being a question anyone has to ask out loud. On a good day
nothing happens and you simply know. On a bad day you have a reason to ring that
is not an accusation, because you already know the answer.

This is what [Remindly](/) does, and it is free. The trade is real and worth
stating plainly: it runs in a web browser on a tablet or old laptop, so the page
has to stay open — think of it as leaving a clock on the counter rather than an
app you open. It needs a working internet connection and a device. A talking
clock needs neither.

## Choosing between them

| | Talking clock | Reminder that reports back |
|---|---|---|
| Speaks aloud at the right time | yes | yes |
| In a familiar recorded voice | **yes** | no |
| Works with no internet | **yes** | no |
| Nothing to keep running | **yes** | no — page must stay open |
| Tells you whether it was done | no | **yes** |
| Change the schedule from anywhere | no | **yes** |
| Cost | around $150 | free |

A rough decision, honestly put:

- **You visit often, or you live with them.** A talking clock. You will see the
  pill box yourself; you do not need reporting.
- **Your parent has dementia, or finds screens upsetting.** A talking clock. The
  familiar voice and the absence of an interface matter more than anything here.
- **You live far away and the daily phone call is wearing you both down.** The
  reporting kind. That call is exactly what it removes.
- **The internet at their house is unreliable.** A talking clock. Do not build a
  routine on a connection you cannot trust.
- **You are not sure yet.** Remindly costs nothing to try, so trying it settles
  the question faster than reading about it.

There is no reason you cannot use both, incidentally. A talking clock for the
medication and something reporting for the appointments is a perfectly sensible
arrangement.

## What neither of them does

Neither can tell you a tablet was swallowed. A clock speaks into an empty room
without knowing it is empty; a button can be pressed by someone walking past.
Any product in this space implying otherwise is overselling itself.

If a missed dose would be genuinely dangerous, neither of these is the right
tool on its own. Ask the pharmacy about blister packs or compliance packaging —
many will prepare them, a lot of families do not know to ask, and it is often
free. A locking automatic pill dispenser is worth looking at too, at a similar
price to a talking clock.

## Where to start

If you have not yet: a weekly pill organiser and a conversation with the
pharmacy. That is cheap, it works, and it may be enough.

If you have done that and you are still making the nine o'clock call — that call
is the thing to solve, and it is the one a talking clock cannot help with.
