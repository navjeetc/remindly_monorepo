---
title: How to know whether your parent actually took their medication
description: The daily phone call to check on a dose is exhausting for both of you, and it does not even work. Four approaches that do, from a pill organiser to a spoken reminder that reports back.
published_on: 2026-07-26
---

There is a phone call a lot of us make around nine in the morning. You already
know how it goes. You ask whether they have had their tablets. They say yes.
You are not sure they have. You ask again in a slightly different way. Now they
are annoyed, because they are a grown adult being checked up on, and you feel
like a warden.

The call is exhausting for both of you. What makes it worse is that it does not
actually work. You are asking someone to remember whether they remembered.

Here are the approaches that do work, roughly in order of how much they cost and
how much they ask of the person taking the medication.

## 1. A weekly pill organiser

The seven-compartment box, filled on a Sunday. It is the cheapest thing on this
list and probably the most effective single item, because it turns an invisible
question into a visible one. Nobody has to remember anything. You look at the
box and the box tells you.

The reason it is not the whole answer: it tells *them*. Unless you are in the
house, you still cannot see the box, and you are back to the phone call. It also
says nothing about *when* — a Tuesday compartment emptied at nine in the evening
looks identical to one emptied at breakfast.

Still: if you do nothing else on this list, do this one.

## 2. An alarm on something they already own

A phone alarm, a kitchen timer, an old-fashioned alarm clock. Free, immediate,
and worth trying before anything more elaborate.

Where this falls down is that an alarm is a noise, not a message. It says
something is due; it does not say what. People get very good at silencing an
alarm while half-asleep and then having no memory of it. And it still reports
nothing back to you.

## 3. A talking reminder clock

There is a whole category of device here — reminder clocks that announce
recorded messages out loud at set times, often in the family member's own
recorded voice. They generally cost somewhere between seventy and a hundred and
fifty pounds or dollars depending on the model, and for a lot of families they
are genuinely the right answer.

The advantages are real: it is a single-purpose object that sits on a shelf, it
needs no wifi, and there is no screen to get lost in. If your parent finds
anything with a screen stressful, a dedicated device is worth the money.

The limitation is the same as the pill organiser's. It speaks at their end. It
does not tell you anything.

## 4. A spoken reminder that reports back

This is the category that closes the loop, and it is the one worth understanding
properly, because it is the only approach on this list that ends the phone call.

The shape of it: reminders are spoken aloud at their end, at the times you set
from your own phone. When the reminder comes up, they mark it done — one large
button — or it goes unmarked. Either way, you find out. Not by asking. It just
appears, on your side, without either of you having to raise it.

What that changes is subtler than it sounds. The question "did you take your
tablets?" stops being a question anyone has to ask. On a good day, nothing
happens and you simply know. On a bad day, you have a reason to ring that is not
an accusation, because you already know the answer — you are ringing because
something is actually wrong, which is a different conversation entirely.

This is what [Remindly](/) does, and it is free to use. It runs in a web browser
on any tablet or old laptop, which is the trade: no special hardware to buy, but
the page does have to stay open on their device, the way a clock sits on a
shelf. If that is a dealbreaker, one of the dedicated devices above is the
better answer, and I would rather say so than sell you something that will
frustrate you.

## What none of this does

Nothing here can tell you a tablet was swallowed. A box can be emptied into a
bin, a button can be pressed by someone walking past, and a device that speaks
into an empty room is speaking into an empty room. Every product in this space
that implies otherwise is overselling itself.

What these approaches actually give you is a much better signal than you have
now, and — more importantly — one that arrives without a daily conversation that
makes your parent feel watched. That is a smaller claim than "peace of mind",
and it is the true one.

If a missed dose would be genuinely dangerous, none of this substitutes for a
pharmacist's blister pack, a carer visit, or a conversation with their doctor.
Ask the pharmacy about compliance packaging — many will do it, a lot of people
do not know to ask, and it is usually free.

## Where to start

If you are at the beginning of this: buy the pill organiser this week, ask the
pharmacy about blister packs, and see how far that gets you. It is a lot.

If you have done that and you are still making the nine o'clock call — that is
the point at which the fourth option starts being worth your time.
