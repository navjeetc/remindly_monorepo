Nobody hands you a list when a parent starts needing looking after. You work it
out in pieces, usually after something has already gone wrong — the prescription
that ran out on a Sunday, the appointment nobody was taking them to, the fridge
with three opened cartons of milk in it.

The worry that follows is rarely *can I do this*. It is *what am I forgetting*.

So here is the list, in the order a day actually happens. There is a
[printable one-page version](/caregiver_checklist) if you would rather have it
on the fridge than in your head — it is free and there is nothing to sign up
for.

## Morning

**Up and dressed.** Not a small thing. Someone who has stopped getting dressed
is telling you something, and it usually predates anything a doctor would pick
up.

**Morning medication.** Usually the dose that matters most, and the one a
disrupted morning takes out first.

**Breakfast — not just tea.** Appetite drifts quietly with age. A parent who
insists they ate can be telling the truth about a biscuit.

**Glasses on, hearing aid in, batteries working.** A flat hearing aid battery
looks exactly like confusion, and gets mistaken for it constantly. It is worth
checking before you conclude anything about someone's memory.

## Through the day

**Something to drink every few hours.** Dehydration in older adults presents as
confusion, unsteadiness and fatigue — the same things everyone worries are
something worse. It is one of the most common causes of a sudden bad day, and
one of the easiest to fix.

**A proper meal in the middle of the day.** For a lot of people this is the meal
that actually gets eaten.

**Out of the chair.** A walk, the garden, up and down the stairs. Strength lost
sitting is disproportionately hard to get back.

**Spoke to somebody other than you.** Isolation does measurable harm, and it
creeps. If the honest answer has been "no" all week, that is worth acting on as
seriously as a missed dose.

## Evening

**Evening meal, evening medication.** The second most-missed dose after the
morning one, usually because evenings are less structured.

**Doors locked, heating set, and anything needed in the night within reach.**
Glass of water, phone, light. Most household falls happen on the way to
something.

## Once a week is enough

These do not need daily attention and will quietly cause a crisis if they never
get any:

- **Medication stock** — reorder before it runs low, not after. Sunday is when
  this bites.
- **Appointments coming up**, and specifically *who is taking them there*.
- **Food in date**, and anything gone off in the fridge.
- **Post opened, bills paid.** Unopened post is one of the earlier visible signs
  that something is slipping.
- **Devices charged** — phone, hearing aids, anything medical.
- **Nothing new to trip over.** Rugs, wires, a box left on the stairs. Falls are
  the single largest cause of a parent's independence ending suddenly, and the
  causes are usually this boring.

## The part no list can tick

Underneath all of it is the question the checklist cannot answer: **are they
doing all right?**

Write down when something seems different. Not a diagnosis — just "quieter than
usual", "did not want to go out", "asked me the same thing twice". A pattern is
obvious on paper and nearly invisible across three weeks of memory, and it is
what makes a GP appointment useful rather than vague.

## Checking without it feeling like supervision

Here is the difficulty with everything above. Every item is something you want
to *know*, and asking directly costs your parent a little of their sense of
being a competent adult.

"Did you eat?" "Did you take your tablets?" "Did you go out today?" — asked
daily, that is not a conversation, it is an inspection. Most families feel this,
stop asking, and swap it for worrying instead.

A few things that help:

**Ask about the day, not the items.** "What did you get up to?" usually returns
most of this list without anyone being examined.

**Let them keep the list too.** A checklist on their fridge, filled in by them,
is a different object from one you hold. Same information, completely different
relationship to it.

**Let something else carry the routine parts.** The medication and appointment
lines are the ones that repeat forever, and they are the ones worth handing off.
That is what [Remindly](/) is for: it speaks each reminder aloud at their end
and tells you what was done and what was missed, so those particular questions
never need asking out loud. It is [free to use](/faq).

## Start smaller than this

If the whole list feels like a lot: it is. Nobody does all of it every day, and
treating it as a standard to meet is a good way to feel like you are failing at
something you are actually doing well.

Print it, put it where you will see it, and let it prompt you. Missing the milk
being off is not a failure. Not noticing for three weeks that nobody has visited
is the thing the list is really for.

*General information from working with families, not medical advice. Anything
about medication, or a change you are worried about, belongs with a pharmacist
or GP.*
